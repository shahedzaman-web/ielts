Reading Passage 3

Manage Your Energy, Not Your Time

Why expanding your energy capacity — not squeezing more hours out of the day — is the key to sustainable high performance

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 27–40, which are based on the reading passage below.

AAt our company, the Energy Project, we have worked with thousands of executives and, with remarkable consistency, they tell us they're pushing themselves harder than ever to keep up. The problem with working longer hours is that time is a finite resource. However, energy is a different story. Energy comes from four main wellsprings: the body, emotions, mind and spirit and, in each area, energy can be systematically expanded. Most organizations invest in developing employees' knowledge and competence but few help build and sustain their energy. In fact, greater energy makes it possible to get more done in less time at a higher level of engagement.

BThe body is the foundation of energy. The human body naturally moves through cycles of roughly ninety to one hundred and twenty minutes, known as ultradian rhythms. At the end of each of these cycles, the body craves recovery. When we ignore these signals — restlessness, difficulty concentrating, irritability — we draw on emergency reserves that eventually deplete us. The obvious remedy is to take short breaks roughly every ninety minutes. The trouble is that taking breaks every hour goes against the culture of most organizations, where long hours are equated with commitment and the only acceptable pause is lunch. Worse still, most people have lost the ability to notice their body's signals in the first place. By re-learning to recognize fatigue and to punctuate the working day with brief intervals of rest, people can sustain far higher levels of concentration and productivity over the long run.

CEmotions are the second wellspring. It has long been understood that people perform better when they feel positive, but what surprises many of our clients is the discovery that they cannot perform well when they are feeling any other way. Negative emotions, in other words, exact a heavy toll: they drain energy, create friction between colleagues and lead to poorer thinking and poorer decisions. Learning to manage emotions is therefore not a soft extra but a core skill. One of the simplest and most powerful practices is the habit of expressing appreciation to others. Our research shows that such expressions have a far greater impact on both giver and receiver when they are fuller and more precise — that is, when they single out a specific action and its effect, rather than offering vague praise. Companies that build regular rituals of appreciation around this principle see measurable improvements in morale and performance.

DThe third wellspring is the mind. Real productivity depends less on the number of hours worked than on the ability to focus on the most important task at hand. Yet most people spend their working day in a state of continuous partial attention, flitting between emails, messages and meetings. The remedy is to build rituals that protect focused work. One executive we worked with moved to an empty conference room whenever he had an important report to write, and found that, by removing distractions, he could complete challenging tasks far more quickly. Others schedule their most demanding work for the time of day when their energy is naturally highest and reserve the rest of the day for more routine activities.

EThe fourth wellspring is the spirit, which draws its energy from a sense of purpose and from living in alignment with one's deepest values. The difficulty is that many people cannot say clearly what their values are. We have found a surprisingly reliable way to help them find out. We ask people to describe the qualities they find most off-putting in others — the behaviours they cannot stand. What people find intolerable in others almost always reveals a value they hold themselves. Once values are articulated, people can begin to allocate their time and energy in ways that serve what matters to them, which in turn makes demanding work feel less draining and more sustaining.

FNot everyone responds to our programmes in the same way. The candidates who do best are those we describe as optimal: people who are eager for solutions and willing to change, but who are not yet completely overwhelmed by their circumstances. Those in the grip of extreme stress tend to lack the capacity to take on new habits, whereas those who are already thriving see little need for them. The individuals below illustrate what sustained practice can achieve.

Gary Faro used to save his most difficult work for the early morning, when he knew his energy was at its peak. After learning to build recovery into his day, his concentration became steady throughout the day, and he no longer needs to schedule his toughest work for a particular time of day.

Fujio Nishida used to smoke a cigarette whenever he felt under pressure. A simple breathing exercise taught in our programme gave him a way to relax in seconds, and he found that he could immediately adopt a positive technique to cope with pressure — and no longer needed the cigarettes.

Ben Jenkins made a point of holding regular dinners with his team in which, instead of talking about work, he acknowledged each person's achievements and invited them to discuss their aspirations — their life and ambitions beyond the workplace.

Dan Cluna was struggling to complete his reports on time. After he began working in a quiet conference room for part of each day, with his phone and email switched off, he found that he could complete challenging tasks more quickly than he ever had at his desk.

Jean Luc Desquesne reorganised his mornings so that he spent the first part of the day on his single most important task, before opening his inbox. The result was that he gets a sense of accomplishment early in the day — often by ten o'clock — which carries him through the remaining hours of work.
