The bar and line charts illustrate average monthly temperatures and bathing suit sales in New York for the first six months of 2012. The units are measured in hundreds of thousands of US dollars and degrees Fahrenheit.

Interestingly, sales peaked in April, when temperatures were still moderate, and then fell sharply as the weather continued to warm — the two trends move in opposite directions after April.

Model answer (Band 7+ style)

The bar chart and line graph compare average monthly temperatures with bathing suit sales in New York during the first six months of 2012. Sales are measured in hundreds of thousands of US dollars, while temperature is given in degrees Fahrenheit.

Overall, both figures rose across the period, though not in parallel. Bathing suit sales fluctuated markedly, peaking in April before falling sharply, whereas the average temperature climbed steadily from January to June.

In the first quarter, bathing suit sales increased gradually from around 10 million dollars in January to 15 million in March. April then saw a dramatic surge to nearly 28 million dollars, the highest point of the whole period. This peak was short-lived, however: sales collapsed to roughly 8 million dollars in May and fell further to about 1 million dollars by June.

Turning to temperature, New York was coldest at the start of the year, with the average holding steady at about 30°F in January and February. It then rose steadily over the following months, reaching roughly 60°F by May and around 65°F by June. Interestingly, the sharpest falls in sales coincided with the warmest months, suggesting that purchases were made in advance of the summer season rather than during it.

Targets for the next Task 1

  1. One number format, every time. Pick "10 million", "$10m" or "10,000,000" and never mix systems in the same paragraph.
  2. Compare the two charts. With a combined chart, one sentence linking the two trends is worth more than a whole extra paragraph of description.
  3. Articles and plurals. The first six months, the line chart, 30 degrees. Do a two-minute check for "the" and final "s" before you submit.
  4. Stop repeating "the figure". Rotate: sales, the temperature, it, they, the number, this figure.
  5. Check your sentences have one subject and one verb. Read the final paragraph aloud — if you lose track of who is doing what, so will the examiner.
