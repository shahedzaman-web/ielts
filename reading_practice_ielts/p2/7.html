Reading Passage 2

Roller coaster: the great fairground attraction

From ice slides to inverted steel giants — the long and thrilling history of the roller coaster

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14–26, which are based on the reading passage below.

Roller coasters are among the most popular attractions at amusement parks around the world. The ride is essentially a specialised railway that employs gravity and inertia to propel a train of cars along a winding track. During the ride, the train is dragged to the top of a lift hill by a chain mechanism, and from there it races along a series of hills, curves and loops, continually converting potential energy into kinetic energy as it descends. Passengers sit in each car, which is secured to the track by wheels or runners.

The modern roller coaster's origins can be traced to the 16th-century ice slides of Russia. Built near St Petersburg, these were steep wooden slopes covered with a face of ice, down which thrill-seekers rode in sleds. Since they had to climb to the top to begin their descent, Catherine the Great is said to have commissioned a special lift to spare her the effort.

The idea eventually spread to France, where the warmer climate made ice slides impractical. Instead, enterprising builders constructed wooden slopes that were waxed to reduce friction, and small wheels were added to the sleds, allowing them to run on flat sections of track as well as downhill. These early French rides are considered the first significant modifications to the basic Russian design.

By the early 19th century, similar rides had appeared in the United States. The first such attraction was built at Mauch Chunk, Pennsylvania, on a former railway used for transporting coal. Initially, the cars were hauled back uphill by mules, but as technology advanced, steam power was introduced to drive the lifts, making the rides faster and more efficient.

The early decades of the twentieth century saw a boom in roller coaster construction. Over 1,500 coasters were operating in the United States alone by 1929. However, the Great Depression of the 1930s, followed by the Second World War, led to a steep decline. Many parks closed, and the majority of coasters fell into disrepair or were demolished.

The industry was revived in the 1970s, thanks to new steel-track technology that allowed for taller, faster and more adventurous designs. This revival, however, was relatively short-lived. For most of the 1980s, the number of new coasters built was lower than in either the previous or the following decade, and attendance at many parks stagnated.

In the 1990s, the industry entered a new phase. Improved computer-aided design enabled engineers to devise ever more varied designs — stand-up, inverted, launched and floorless coasters among them. The 1990s thus became one of the most productive and inventive periods in the history of roller coaster design, and by the end of the decade the attraction had firmly re-established itself as the fairground's greatest spectacle.
