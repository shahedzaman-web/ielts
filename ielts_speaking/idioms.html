Choosing the Right Idiom

Don't memorise idioms as isolated vocabulary. Group them by communicative purpose and by the situations you're likely to describe in the exam.

🧩

Why collocations matter

An idiom is only half the job — examiners notice whether the words around it sound natural. Learning chunks such as get the hang of it, a soft spot for or lend somebody a hand makes the expression easier to recall and much harder to get wrong.

Part 1

Favour short, conversational expressions — not my cup of tea, give it a shot, once in a blue moon, under the weather.

Part 2

Use an idiom when telling a story or describing a change, difficulty, surprise, preference or personal experience.

Part 3

Choose neutral, analytical language first. Add an idiom only when it fits naturally with your argument or example.

⚙️

Grammar & Fixed-Expression Accuracy

Most idiom errors are not vocabulary errors — they are control errors. The meaning is known, but the article, preposition, tense or fixed word order changes.

Area What to control
📌

Common source-list corrections

  • Killed two birds with one stone is correct for one specific past action; the base form kill two birds with one stone is correct in general statements. Match the tense to the sentence.
  • Dogs and cats is understandable, but raining cats and dogs is the conventional fixed form.
  • Bite the bullet, a piece of cake, go the extra mile and other fixed expressions keep their normal articles and structure.
  • Several traditional origin stories are simplified or disputed. Treat origins as memory cues, not facts to reproduce.
⚠️

Common Pitfalls

Relevance beats decoration. A cluster of expressions can sound rehearsed rather than fluent.

🚫

Memorised-script warning: the safest way to use an idiom is to remember the meaning and a typical situation — not one fixed sentence that must appear in every answer.

🔁

Practice Method

The objective is active recall: you should be able to choose an expression spontaneously because the situation triggers the meaning.
