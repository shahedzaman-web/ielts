What Is “Right Form of Verbs”?

“Right form of verbs” is the grammar skill of choosing the correct form of a verb for a given sentence — base, -s, past, past participle, -ing, or infinitive. The choice depends on the subject, the tense, the sentence type (conditional, wish, inversion…), and the words around the gap.

💡 Core idea: before you choose the form, ask three questions — 1. Who is the subject? (singular / plural, person) 2. What time is the sentence about? (past / present / future) 3. Is there a trigger word? (since, wish, if, lest, hardly, enjoy, to…) The answers almost always determine the form.

Verb formWhen to useExample
Base formafter modals, to-infinitives, imperativesHe can swim. / I want to go.
-s / -es formthird person singular, present simpleShe writes every day.
Past simplefinished past actionsHe wrote a letter yesterday.
Past participleperfect tenses, passive voiceHe has written. / It was written.
-ing formafter prepositions, after enjoy/mind etc., continuous tensesHe is writing. / I enjoy reading.
Infinitive (to + base)after want/hope/decide, after adjectivesI want to go. / It is easy to learn.

The 20 Rules — In Detail

Every rule includes the principle, examples, and the trap that costs marks in exams.

1 Subject–Verb Agreement

A singular subject takes a singular verb (with -s in the present simple); a plural subject takes the base form.

He goes to school. / They go to school.
The news is good. / The students are ready.
⚠️ Watch out: words between the subject and verb do not change the agreement — “The box of chocolates is on the table” (subject = box, not chocolates).

2 Universal Truth & Habitual Fact → Present Simple

Scientific facts, universal truths, and habitual actions always take the present simple, even inside a past-tense sentence.

Water boils at 100°C.
The sun rises in the east.
He goes to school every day.
⚠️ Watch out: “The teacher told us that the earth moves around the sun” — the reported clause keeps the present because it is a universal truth.

3 Past Narration — Past Follows Past

When the main clause is in the past, the subordinate clause is also normally in the past (past simple or past perfect for an earlier action).

He said that he was ill.
When I arrived, he was watching TV.
She told me she had read the book.
After he had eaten dinner, he went to bed.
⚠️ Watch out: universal truths and habitual facts are exceptions — “He said that water boils at 100°C.”

4 Perfect Tenses — since, for, already, just, yet, ever, never

These trigger words almost always require a perfect tense: present perfect (have/has + past participle) or present perfect continuous (have/has been + -ing).

TriggerTenseExample
since / forpresent perfect (continuous)I have lived here since 2010.
already / just / yetpresent perfectHe has already finished.
ever / neverpresent perfect (experience)She has never been to Paris.
so far / recently / latelypresent perfectI have not seen him lately.
before + past pointpast perfectShe had left before I arrived.
⚠️ Watch out: “yesterday” and “last week” are finished past — use past simple, not present perfect. “I have seen him yesterday” ✗ → “I saw him yesterday” ✓.

5 Conditional Sentences

TypeIf-clauseMain clauseExample
Zero (fact)present simplepresent simpleIf you heat ice, it melts.
First (real future)present simplewill + baseIf it rains, we will stay home.
Second (unreal present)past simplewould + baseIf I had money, I would travel.
Third (unreal past)past perfectwould have + ppIf she had studied, she would have passed.
Mixedpast perfectwould + base (now)If I had saved money, I would be rich now.
⚠️ Watch out: never use will in the if-clause — “If it will rain” ✗ → “If it rains” ✓. Unless = “if not”.

6 Modal Verbs + Base Form

After can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, ought to, had better, would rather, need not, dare not — use the base form (no -s, no -ed, no -ing).

He can swim. / You must obey the rules.
She should be careful. / They may come tomorrow.
You had better leave now. / I would rather stay home.
⚠️ Watch out: “ought” and “used” take to — “You ought to obey”.

7 Causative Verbs — make, let, have, get, help

VerbPatternExample
makemake + object + bare infinitiveHe made me tell the truth.
letlet + object + bare infinitiveLet him answer the question.
havehave + object + bare infinitiveI had the mechanic repair my car.
getget + object + to-infinitiveI got him to help me.
helphelp + object + (to) infinitiveShe helped me (to) finish the work.
have / get + object + ppcausative passiveI had my hair cut. / She got her car repaired.
⚠️ Watch out: get takes to-infinitive; make, let, have do not.

8 Verbs of Perception — see, hear, watch, notice, feel

These take either the bare infinitive (complete action) or the -ing form (ongoing action).

I saw him cross the road. (I saw the whole action)
I saw him crossing the road. (I saw him while he was crossing)
She heard the baby cry. / She heard the baby crying.
⚠️ In passive: use the to-infinitive — “He was seen to enter the room.”

9 Wish / As if / As though — Past Subjunctive “were”

After wish, as if, as though, and in unreal conditions, use were for all subjects, and past simple for a present wish.

I wish I were rich.
He talks as if he knew everything.
She behaves as though she were the boss.
I wish I had more time. (past simple for present unreal)
⚠️ Watch out: “I wish I was tall” is common in speech, but exams expect “I wish I were tall”.

10 It is high time / It is time / Would rather / Had better

StructureFormExample
It is high time / It is time+ subject + past simpleIt is high time we left.
It is time (no subject)+ to-infinitiveIt is time to go.
would rather (same subject)+ base formI would rather stay home.
would rather (different subject)+ subject + past simpleI would rather you left now.
had better+ base formYou had better see a doctor.
⚠️ Watch out: “It is high time we leave” ✗ → “It is high time we left” ✓.

11 Gerund (-ing) after Prepositions & Certain Verbs

After prepositions (in, on, at, of, for, with, without, before, after, by, from, about, despite, in spite of…) and after verbs like enjoy, mind, avoid, finish, stop, suggest, recommend, consider, keep, deny, admit, postpone, risk, imagine, practise — use the -ing form.

He is good at singing.
She left without saying goodbye.
I enjoy reading novels.
They apologised for being late.
⚠️ Watch out: “look forward to” ends with to, but it is a preposition — “I look forward to seeing you” (not “to see”).

12 Infinitive (to + base) after Certain Verbs & Adjectives

PatternExamples
verb + to-infinitivewant, need, hope, expect, plan, decide, promise, refuse, agree, offer, manage, afford, learn, forget
adjective + to-infinitivehappy, glad, sad, easy, difficult, hard, ready, eager, able, willing, afraid
too + adjective + to-infinitivetoo tired to walk
adjective + enough + to-infinitiveold enough to drive
in order to / so as toin order to pass
I want to be a doctor.
It is easy to learn English.
⚠️ Watch out: stop changes meaning — “stop smoking” (quit) vs “stop to smoke” (pause in order to smoke).

13 used to / be used to / get used to

StructureMeaningExample
used to + basepast habit (no longer true)He used to smoke.
be used to + -ingbe accustomed toShe is used to getting up early.
get used to + -ingbecome accustomed toHe will get used to working here.
⚠️ Watch out: “I used to live in London” (past habit) ≠ “I am used to living in London” (accustomed).

14 Passive Voice — be + Past Participle

Passive = form of be + past participle (V3). The tense is shown by be.

The letter was sent yesterday.
English is spoken all over the world.
The results will be announced tomorrow.
⚠️ Watch out: intransitive verbs (happen, arrive, occur, die, rise) have no passive — “An accident was happened” ✗ → “An accident happened” ✓.

15 lest + should

After lest, use should + base form (or the bare subjunctive).

Study hard lest you should fail.
Hurry up lest you should miss the train.
Take an umbrella lest it should rain.
⚠️ Watch out: never use will or would after lest — “lest you will fail” ✗.

16 No sooner…than / Hardly…when / Scarcely…when

These use past perfect + inversion in the first clause, and past simple in the second.

StructureConjunctionExample
No sooner had + S + pp …thanNo sooner had he arrived than it started raining.
Hardly had + S + pp …whenHardly had I entered when the phone rang.
Scarcely had + S + pp …whenScarcely had he left when it began to rain.
⚠️ Watch out: “No sooner” pairs with than, not when. “Hardly / Scarcely” pair with when, not than.

17 Each / Every / One of + Singular Verb

Each, every, one of, either, neither (as subject) take a singular verb.

Each of the boys has a book.
Every student needs to submit the form.
One of my friends is coming today.
⚠️ Watch out: “One of my friends is coming” — the verb agrees with one, not friends.

18 The number of / A number of

ExpressionVerbExample
The number of + plural nounsingularThe number of students is increasing.
A number of + plural nounpluralA number of students are absent.
⚠️ Watch out: the number of = “the total” (singular); a number of = “several” (plural).

19 Either…or / Neither…nor — Proximity Rule

The verb agrees with the subject closest to it.

Neither he nor his friends are coming. (nearest = friends)
Neither his friends nor he is coming. (nearest = he)
Either the teacher or the students are responsible.
Either the students or the teacher is responsible.

20 Inversion after Negative Adverbs

When a sentence begins with never, seldom, rarely, hardly, scarcely, little, nowhere, not only, no sooner, the auxiliary verb comes before the subject (inversion).

Never have I seen such a sight.
Seldom does he go to the cinema.
Hardly had she finished when the bell rang.
Not only did he sing, but he also danced.
⚠️ Watch out: do not forget the auxiliary — “Never I have seen” ✗ → “Never have I seen” ✓.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose the right form of a verb in a gap-fill?

Ask three questions: (1) Who is the subject — singular or plural? (2) What time is the sentence about — past, present or future? (3) Is there a trigger word (since, wish, if, lest, hardly, enjoy, to)? The answers almost always determine whether you need the base form, -s form, past simple, past participle, -ing form or to-infinitive.

What is the difference between “I saw him cross” and “I saw him crossing”?

With verbs of perception, the bare infinitive (“cross”) means you saw the whole action from start to finish. The -ing form (“crossing”) means you saw the action in progress — you caught part of it. Both are grammatical; the choice depends on what you want to emphasise.

Why is “It is high time we leave” wrong?

The structure “It is high time / It is time” is followed by a past simple verb, not the present. So the correct form is “It is high time we left.” If there is no subject, use the to-infinitive: “It is time to leave.”

What is the difference between “the number of” and “a number of”?

The number of means “the total” and takes a singular verb — “The number of students is increasing.” A number of means “several” and takes a plural verb — “A number of students are absent.”

When do I use “lest”?

Lest means “in case” or “so that … not” and is followed by should + base form (or the bare subjunctive). Example: “Study hard lest you should fail.” It is formal and mostly found in exams and literature, rarely in casual speech.
