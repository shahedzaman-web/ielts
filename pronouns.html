What Are Pronouns?

Pronouns are words you substitute for nouns when the reader already knows which noun you mean. They make speech and writing faster and avoid repetition. The noun a pronoun replaces is called its antecedent.

💡 Core idea: a pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number (singular / plural), gender (he / she / it / they), and person (first / second / third).

Sarah has always loved fashion. She announced that she wants to study design.
The students finished early, so they went home.

The 20 Rules — In Detail

Every rule includes the principle, extra explanation, real examples, and the traps examiners love to test.

1 Personal Pronouns — Subject & Object

Personal pronouns change form based on person, number and case. Subject pronouns do the action; object pronouns receive it.

PersonSubjectObject
1st singularIme
2nd singular/pluralyouyou
3rd singularhe / she / ithim / her / it
1st pluralweus
3rd pluraltheythem
I brought all the snacks. We weren't planning on staying over.
She called me and asked us to join them.
⚠️ Watch out: after be in formal writing use the subject pronoun — “It was I”, not “It was me” (though “me” is common in speech).

2 Possessive Adjectives vs Possessive Pronouns

Possessive adjectives sit before a noun. Possessive pronouns stand alone and replace the noun entirely.

Possessive adjectivePossessive pronoun
mymine
youryours
his / her / itshis / hers / its
ourours
theirtheirs
That is my book. That book is mine.
Their car is new, but ours is older.
⚠️ Watch out: possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe — yours, not your's; its, not it's (= it is).

3 Reflexive & Intensive Pronouns

Reflexive pronouns end in -self / -selves. They are used (a) when the subject and object are the same, or (b) for emphasis (intensive).

SubjectReflexive
Imyself
youyourself / yourselves
he / she / ithimself / herself / itself
weourselves
theythemselves
Steve introduced himself to the other guests. (reflexive)
I repaired it myself. (intensive)
⚠️ Watch out: do not use a reflexive after verbs like feel, relax, concentrate, meet, wash, shave, dress in normal usage — “I feel good”, not “I feel myself good”.

4 Demonstrative Pronouns

This / that / these / those point to specific things. Near vs far; singular vs plural.

SingularPlural
Nearthisthese
Farthatthose
This has no return address.
Those aren't swans, they're geese.
I like these better than those.

5 Indefinite Pronouns

Refer to non-specific people or things. Most -body / -one / -thing words are singular.

TypePronounsVerb
Peopleanybody, somebody, nobody, everybody, anyone, someone, everyonesingular
Thingsanything, something, nothing, everythingsingular
Placesanywhere, somewhere, nowhere, everywheresingular
Everybody was late to work.
Nobody knows the trouble I've seen.
⚠️ Watch out: “Everybody has their own opinion” is accepted in modern usage — the verb stays singular; only the possessive pronoun may shift to their.

6 Relative Pronouns — who / whom / which / that / whose

Introduce a relative clause and link it to the noun it describes.

PronounUse forExample
whopeople (subject)The woman who called didn't leave a message.
whompeople (object)The man whom I met was kind.
whichthings / animalsThe car which is parked outside is mine.
thatpeople or things (defining clauses)The book that I bought is excellent.
whosepossessionThe girl whose bag was stolen cried.
The woman who called earlier didn't leave a message.
My car, which is twenty years old, still runs well.
⚠️ Watch out: do not use that in a non-defining (comma) clause — “My car, which is old, …”, not “My car, that is old, …”.

7 Interrogative Pronouns

Used to ask questions: who, whom, whose, what, which.

Who wants a bag of jelly beans?
What is your name?
Which do you prefer — tea or coffee?
Whose is this umbrella?
⚠️ Watch out: who = subject; whom = object. “Who called you?” (subject) vs “Whom did you call?” (object).

8 Reciprocal Pronouns — each other / one another

Show a mutual relationship. Use each other for two, and one another for more than two (though the distinction is fading in modern English).

Javier and Priya are competing with each other.
The five team members congratulated one another.
⚠️ Watch out: use reciprocal pronouns only when the action is mutual. “They love each other” means A loves B and B loves A.

9 Distributive Pronouns — each, either, neither, none, any

Refer to individuals within a group. Most are singular.

Peanut butter or chocolate? I'm good with either.
There were four cats, and none had long hair.
Each of the boys has a medal.
⚠️ Watch out: each / either / neither / one as subject take a singular verb — “Neither of the answers is correct”.

10 there vs it

There says something exists. It refers to a specific thing, place, fact, or situation.

There

There's a new restaurant on Hill Street.
There was a lot of traffic.

It

It's very good. (the restaurant)
It was a complete surprise.
💡 Test: if you can ask “Does X exist?” → use there. If you can point to X → use it.

11 some vs any

Some

Positive sentences, offers, requests, when we expect “yes”.

We bought some flowers.
Can I have some sugar?

Any

Negative sentences, most questions, if-clauses, “it doesn't matter which”.

We didn't buy any flowers.
Do you have any luggage?
You can take any bus.

12 no, none, nothing, nobody

No + noun; none without a noun; nothing / nobody / nowhere = not + anything / anybody / anywhere.

We had no bus. / We had none. / I said nothing.
Nobody knows. / Nowhere to go.
⚠️ Watch out: do not use a double negative — “I didn't say anything” (not “I didn't say nothing”).

13 much, many, little, few

CountableUncountable
many / fewmany friends, few cars—
much / little—much time, little money
a lot of / plenty ofa lot of friends, plenty of ideasa lot of luck, plenty of time
We didn't spend much money. / Many people drive too fast.
He has little time. / She has few friends.
💡 Tip: a few / a little = some (positive); few / little = not enough (negative). “He has a few friends” vs “He has few friends”.

14 all, most, no, none + of

Use of before the, my, these, those and before pronouns (us, you, them).

Some of the people I work with are strange.
None of this money is mine.
All of us were late.
⚠️ Watch out: no of before a noun without a determiner — “All cars have wheels”, not “All of cars”.

15 both, neither, either (for two)

Both

the two

Both restaurants are good.

Neither

not one nor the other

Neither restaurant is expensive.

Either

one or the other

We can go to either restaurant.
Both of these restaurants are good. / Neither of them was expensive.
⚠️ Watch out: both takes a plural verb; neither and either take a singular verb — “Both are good” / “Neither is good”.

16 all, every, whole

all

+ noun or all of

All my friends were happy.

every

+ singular noun

Every seat was taken.

whole

+ singular noun (complete)

I read the whole book.
Everybody was happy. / Everything went wrong.

17 each vs every

Each

individual, one by one — for two or more.

Study each sentence carefully.
Each player has three cards.

Every

group, all — for three or more; frequency.

Every window was open.
I go there every day.
💡 Test: with exactly two items, use each, not every — “Each of my two hands”, not “every of my two hands”.

18 Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement

A pronoun must match its antecedent in number and gender. Singular nouns take singular pronouns; plural nouns take plural pronouns.

The dog wagged its tail. (singular → its)
The dogs wagged their tails. (plural → their)
Each student must bring his or her own laptop. (or their in modern usage)
⚠️ Watch out: with collective nouns, choose singular if the group acts as one unit, plural if members act separately — “The team won its match” vs “The team argued among themselves”.

19 Who vs Whom + pronouns after than / as

Who = subject, whom = object. In informal speech who is often used for both, but formal writing and exams expect the distinction.

RolePronounExample
SubjectwhoWho called you?
ObjectwhomWhom did you call?
After prepositionwhomTo whom should I speak?
PossessivewhoseWhose bag is this?
She is taller than I (am). (subject: than I am)
I like him more than her. (object: more than I like her)
💡 Test: substitute he/him. If he fits, use who. If him fits, use whom.

20 of mine / yours + one / ones

A friend of mine = one of my friends. Use of + possessive pronoun after a noun to avoid a clumsy double possessive.

I'm meeting a friend of mine tonight.
We met a relative of yours.
Jason borrowed a book of mine.
⚠️ Watch out: use one / ones to avoid repeating a countable noun — “I don't want the red shirt; I want the blue one.”
📌
How to choose a pronoun: (1) Is it subject or object? (2) Is it singular or plural? (3) Does it need to agree with an antecedent? (4) Is it a fixed collocation (some / any, there / it, both / neither)? Answer those four and the right pronoun almost always appears.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a pronoun and an antecedent?

A pronoun stands in for a noun (he, she, it, they). The antecedent is the noun the pronoun replaces. In “Sarah loves fashion; she studies design”, Sarah is the antecedent and she is the pronoun.

When do I use “who” and when “whom”?

Use who when the pronoun is the subject of a verb (“Who called?”). Use whom when it is the object (“Whom did you call?”) or follows a preposition (“To whom should I speak?”). The quick test: replace with he for who, and him for whom.

Why do we say “a friend of mine” and not “a friend of me”?

“A friend of mine” is the fixed double-possessive pattern. After of, English uses a possessive pronoun (mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs), never an object pronoun. “A friend of me” is not grammatical.

What is the difference between “each other” and “one another”?

Traditionally each other is for two people and one another for more than two. In modern usage the distinction is fading, and either form is often accepted, but exam boards still test the traditional rule.

Why is “everybody has their own opinion” accepted now?

Everybody is grammatically singular (it takes a singular verb — has), but English has no gender-neutral singular possessive pronoun, so their is used as a singular “they”. This is standard in modern usage; the verb still stays singular.
