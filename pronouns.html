What Are Pronouns?

Pronouns are words (or phrases) you substitute for nouns when your reader or listener already knows which noun you're referring to. They make communication faster and avoid repetition.

💡 Antecedent: The noun that a pronoun refers to is called its antecedent.

Sarah has always loved fashion. She announced that she wants to go to fashion school.

Reflexive & Intensive Pronouns

End in -self or -selves. Reflexive: object refers back to subject. Intensive: adds emphasis.

She checked herself out of the hotel. (reflexive)
I built this house myself. (intensive)

Demonstrative Pronouns

Point to specific things: this, that, these, those.

This has no return address. Those aren't swans, they're geese.

Indefinite Pronouns

Refer to non-specific people or things: anybody, everyone, nothing, some etc.

Everybody was late to work. Nobody knows the trouble I've seen.

Relative Pronouns

Introduce relative clauses: who, whom, which, that, what.

The woman who called earlier didn't leave a message.
My car, which is twenty years old, still runs well.

Interrogative Pronouns

Used in questions: who, whom, whose, what, which.

Who wants a bag of jelly beans? What is your name?

Reciprocal Pronouns

each other and one another – show mutual relationships.

Javier and Priya are competing with each other.

Distributive Pronouns

Refer to individuals in a group: either, each, neither, any, none.

Peanut butter or chocolate? I'm good with either.
There were four cats, and none had long hair.

Specific Pronoun Usage

Reflexive / Intensive: myself, yourself, himself etc.

Use reflexive pronouns when the subject and object are the same. Use intensive for emphasis.

Steve introduced himself to the other guests. (reflexive)
I repaired it myself. (intensive)

Do not use reflexive after feel, relax, concentrate, meet, wash, shave, dress (normally).

there and it

There

There's a new restaurant in Hill Street.
There was a lot of traffic.

It

It's very good. (the restaurant)
It was a complete surprise.

Use there to say something exists. Use it for a specific thing, place, fact, situation.

some and any

Some

Positive sentences, offers, requests, when we expect "yes".

We bought some flowers.
Can I have some sugar?

Any

Negative sentences, questions (most), if clauses, "it doesn't matter which".

We didn't buy any flowers.
Do you have any luggage?
You can take any bus.

no, none, any, nothing, nobody

No + noun; none without noun; nothing/nobody/nowhere = not + anything/anybody/anywhere.

We had no bus. / We had none. / I said nothing.
Nobody knows. / Nowhere to go.

much, many, little, few

CountableUncountable
many / fewmany friends, few cars—
much / little—much time, little money
a lot of / plenty ofa lot of friends, plenty of ideasa lot of luck, plenty of time
We didn't spend much money. / Many people drive too fast.
He has little time. / She has few friends.

all, most, no, none etc. + of

Use of before the, my, these, those and before pronouns (us, you, them).

Some of the people I work with are strange.
None of this money is mine.
All of us were late.

But: All cars have wheels (no of before a noun without determiner).

both, neither, either (for two)

both = the two
Both restaurants are good.
neither = not one nor the other
Neither restaurant is expensive.
either = one or the other
We can go to either restaurant.
Both of these restaurants are good. / Neither of them was expensive.

all, every, whole

all + noun (or all of)
All my friends were happy.
every + singular noun
Every seat was taken.
whole + singular noun (complete)
I read the whole book.
Everybody was happy. / Everything went wrong.

each and every

Each

individual, one by one, for two or more.

Study each sentence carefully.
Each player has three cards.

Every

group, all, for three or more, frequency.

Every window was open.
I go there every day.
